# Saad's Editing Style - Evidence-Based Playbook

> Companion pages: [how Saad edits, observed session by session](editing-technique-observed.md) · [learning an editor's style from finished timelines](learning-an-editors-style.md) · [the CapCut hub](capcut.md) · the anonymised [CapCut style brain](../Reference/capcut_style_brain_anonymised.md). Project, client and system names in this public copy are replaced with neutral descriptions; every rule and number is unchanged.

**Updated:** 31 August 2026  
**Status:** Working house style learned from finished CapCut work.

This playbook is based on 77 readable CapCut fingerprints, 50 high-confidence saved export ranges, the two user-confirmed final timelines of a corporate event recap, representative final exports, the training-series correction log, and Saad's direct approvals and rejections. It describes decisions that survived into finished work. It is not a list of generic editing advice.

## 1. The governing principle

Saad edits for **clear story and felt momentum**, not maximum clip coverage. A technically complete first assembly is not finished if it still needs him to decide what the viewer should notice, when a section has ended, or how long an important moment needs to breathe.

The final export range and the final approved timeline outrank:

- the project's whole draft duration;
- unused media and muted experiments left in the timeline;
- an earlier AI-generated plan;
- a root/main timeline when Saad names a different timeline as approved.

## 2. Event and social edits

### Story before inventory

Group related visuals into chapters. Do not scatter the same activity across the reel merely because several attractive clips exist. The audience should feel that the event is moving forward.

Typical chapter logic across approved work:

1. Establish the place or occasion.
2. Introduce people and atmosphere.
3. Move through activations or product details.
4. Enter the formal programme.
5. Alternate speakers/actions with listener or reaction B-roll.
6. Deliver recognition, emotional or ceremonial payoff.
7. End with a clean group, branded or narrative resolution.

The exact chapter order belongs to the approved edit, not to a rigid template. A corporate event recap proves this: Saad's final order is arrival and activations -> venue -> a hands-on guest activity -> speakers -> a second guest activation -> panel -> recognition -> group -> branded outro.

### Pace

- Premium event reels commonly cut around **0.9-1.8 seconds per shot**.
- The approved 60-shot spine of a corporate event recap has a **0.967-second median**.
- Day 2 of a two-day luxury product event has a **0.967-second median**; day 1 is **1.217 seconds**; a farewell tribute is **1.5 seconds**.
- Fast does not mean uniform. Important emotional, informational or ceremonial moments receive longer holds.
- The strongest part of the source clip is selected, often well inside the recording rather than at its start.

### Transitions

Hard cuts are the default. Transitions mark a chapter, soften a tonal move, or close the film. They are not decoration between every pair of shots.

In the approved main sequence of the corporate event recap, only seven of the 59 joins use transitions: Cross Fade, Camera Glow, Fade Shift and Slow Fade. The luxury product event and the farewell tribute also use very few transitions relative to their shot counts. More effect-heavy work such as a corporate product commercial is a separate style family and must not become the default for event reels.

### Shot selection and sequencing

- Prefer the one shot that best communicates the idea over several merely similar shots.
- Alternate scale and function: wide context -> human medium -> detail -> reaction.
- Do not repeat a principal person without a new narrative reason.
- Place audience/reaction B-roll between speakers or formal beats so the programme feels continuous rather than like a row of podium shots.
- Preserve meaningful human micro-moments even when they are imperfect; they are often the emotional bridge.
- Product and activation coverage should feel discovered, not checked off.
- Authentic event footage outranks synthetic replacement imagery. Corrections on the luxury product event established that AI-generated jewellery or booth stills read like fake e-commerce content inside a real event recap.

### Reframing and format adaptation

Square is a new edit pass, not an automatic crop.

- The event recap's Story (9:16) and Square (1:1) timelines share the same 60-shot timing and order.
- The Square timeline manually reframes every main shot for 1:1, normally around 1.78x scale and sometimes much more for the subject.
- Story uses the native vertical frame more often, with selective punch-ins and layered overlays.
- The Story and Square versions use different outro source clips chosen for the destination format.
- Subject position, headroom, gesture and screen text are checked on every shot.

### Sound

- Music must match the cultural and emotional identity of the film, not merely provide tempo.
- Natural sound is retained when it gives the viewer a real human or tactile moment.
- Duck the music around that moment, then restore it cleanly.
- Music edits and chapter edits support each other; the picture is not simply cut to every beat.
- Muted experiments left in a CapCut project do not count as final sound design.

### Look and finish

- Clean, controlled colour is preferred over aggressive stylisation.
- The approved event recap uses a restrained clean/cool filter stack and a short opening blur treatment.
- Branding is allowed to breathe at the end; the outro is not another rapid montage beat.

## 3. Interview, tribute and occasion edits

- Start with a clear emotional question or occasion premise.
- Use interview clips for meaning, then cover them with visual metaphors or supporting human scenes.
- Return to faces often enough that the film remains personal.
- Build to a collective or legacy statement, then resolve with the occasion card and brand.
- A Father's Day film uses more transitions and layered text because it is a designed occasion film; this must not be copied wholesale into raw event coverage.
- When generated supporting scenes are used, lighting, season, eyeline and camera level must remain consistent across the whole film. That film's approved direction uses a warm unified look, an emotional silhouette opener and OTS compositions in which the father visibly watches the action.
- The farewell tribute keeps a brisk 1.5-second visual median but slows footage to roughly 0.8x and uses only a few fades, giving dignity without becoming static.
- A hero physical action must be filmed or convincingly generated as motion. Do not fake cloth, smoke, water or another object wrapping a 3D form by sliding a flat composite over a still; a rejected proof of a cloth sliding off a sports car established this clearly.

## 4. Training-video edits

### What Claude did well

Claude supplied valuable production infrastructure and first-pass labour at scale:

- source analysis, transcription and script cleanup;
- paced screen-recording builds and replacement voice workflows;
- initial call-outs, captions, titles, intros and outros;
- consistent naming and batch assembly across the series;
- local verification tools, contact sheets, issue detection and progress tracking.

This is meaningful work. It reduced the blank-page and repetitive-production burden.

### Why Saad still attributes about 60% to his own finishing

The monitored sessions show that the first builds repeatedly needed human editorial and domain judgement. Across ten monitored training-video projects, the raw edit watcher recorded 153 repositioning actions, 200 clip-shortening actions and 54 holds, plus extensive ripple moves, resizing and text work. These are action events, not unique final decisions, so they must not be converted directly into a percentage. They nevertheless show that the correction pass was substantial rather than cosmetic.

Saad supplied the decisions that made the series usable:

- chose one module as the series benchmark and defined the final recipe;
- corrected overly fast reading moments;
- added or re-timed freezes on dense screens, including 13-second holds where the learner needed them;
- re-struck a call-out when an action repeated instead of holding one box indefinitely;
- moved boxes onto the actual control and kept labels in nearby empty space;
- removed duplicates and overlaps;
- slowed and clarified role changes as scene changes;
- corrected domain wording, narration joins and tail gaps;
- performed the final export and delivery checks;
- decided which automation changes were safe to retain and which were not.

### Training-video timing and call-out rules

- Call-out hold: about **2.1 seconds** by default.
- Freeze hold: about **2.2 seconds** by default.
- Dense screen: **6 seconds or more** when reading requires it.
- A click can precede its narration by up to about **5 seconds** if the visual step remains clear.
- Repeated action: re-strike the call-out as a fresh short hit.
- Role change: treat it as a scene change with a stable screen, named role and readable beat.
- Avoid overlapping boxes.
- Cover the whole target control, not a nearby approximation.
- Use Poppins and the approved blue call-out system.

### Current contribution model

Saad's current project-level assessment is the governing baseline:

- **Claude: approximately 40%** - research, automation, first assembly, repeatable production and assistance.
- **Saad: approximately 60%** - taste, domain accuracy, corrective editing, final timing, QA, approval and delivery.

Do not claim a different percentage without a project-by-project comparison of the AI handoff against the approved result.

## 5. Rules for future AI-assisted editing

1. Study the closest approved final before creating a cut.
2. Build in a duplicate/review timeline; preserve Saad's timeline read-only.
3. Present the edit as a first assembly until Saad approves it.
4. Separate objective assembly work from taste decisions in the handoff.
5. Use a chapter map before choosing clips.
6. Explain why repeated shots remain; otherwise remove them.
7. Reframe each destination format individually.
8. Use transitions only for structural punctuation.
9. Keep natural sound moments and automate the music around them.
10. Verify the actual export, not only the timeline data.
11. For training videos, deliver correction assets and timestamps; do not place replacement audio into Saad's timeline unless he explicitly asks.
12. Record Saad's final changes as the new authority.

## 7. Full-library revision — 31 August 2026

The entire local CapCut corpus has now been structurally read: **85 project folders, 95 timelines, 86 readable timelines and 9 encrypted/unreadable timelines**. Eleven explicitly supplied approved exports were also technically and visually read locally, and all 20 finished training-module exports were verified against their final timeline ranges.

This wider evidence separates the house style into distinct families rather than applying one reel recipe everywhere:

- **Fast event recap:** chapters and human flow first; roughly 0.9–1.8s median cuts; hard cuts lead.
- **Premium product event:** real-event authenticity plus tactile product desire; roughly 0.9–1.3s median cuts; selective slow motion.
- **Tribute/farewell:** brisk but dignified, human exchanges and group legacy; roughly 1.3–1.8s median cuts.
- **Designed occasion/interview:** question/testimony/supporting metaphor/legacy/occasion card; roughly 3–5s meaning-led beats.
- **Corporate commercial:** copy and premise-led, with deliberate visual variation and designed transitions; roughly 4.5–7s visual ideas.
- **Training tutorial:** comprehension-led action, accurate call-out, freeze and confirmation rather than montage rhythm.

**Correction to the earlier training-video default-freeze wording:** the full 20-module final corpus has a roughly **3.0-second median freeze**, not 2.2 seconds. Use 3 seconds as the normal starting point, then extend dense screens to 6 seconds or more when the learner needs it. The 2.1-second call-out default remains correct.
